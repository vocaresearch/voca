William Cranch Draper Sr. (1781–1851), a pioneering American chemist and photographer, is often credited as one of the first to use photography for scientific purposes. In 1839, just months after the invention of the daguerreotype, he captured one of the earliest known photographic portraits of a human face—his own. More importantly, he was among the first to apply photography to scientific documentation, including detailed images of the human eye and the solar spectrum. His work in spectroscopy helped establish the use of light analysis to study chemical composition, a cornerstone of modern astronomy. His son, William Cranch Draper Jr. (1811–1882), followed in his footsteps with even greater scientific ambition. A trained physician and chemist, Draper Jr. made significant contributions to the field of photography, particularly in the development of the wet plate collodion process, which greatly improved image clarity and exposure time. He also conducted groundbreaking work in spectroscopy, producing some of the first detailed solar spectra and helping to identify elements in the Sun’s atmosphere—long before the invention of the spectroscope as we know it today. Both men were deeply connected to Harvard: Draper Sr. was a professor at Harvard Medical School, and Draper Jr. studied there before pursuing his scientific career. Their shared background in Massachusetts and Harvard underscores a legacy of scientific excellence rooted in American academia. Together, their innovations in capturing and analyzing light transformed how we observe and understand the universe. Their work directly influenced later astronomers like William Henry Fox Talbot, Henry Draper (no relation), and eventually the development of astrophotography and spectroscopic analysis in modern astronomy. In short, the Draper family’s legacy is a shining example of how scientific curiosity, combined with technological innovation, can illuminate the cosmos.
